1 Part 1 · Introduction

About RoboSketch-RL

 ▸

Imagine a robot arm holding a pen. You show it a picture of a circle. Nobody tells the robot how to move — it tries, looks at what it drew through a camera, notices "that's not a circle yet," and tries again. After many attempts it learns to draw a clean circle by itself. That is RoboSketch-RL.

1.1 What is the project, in one paragraph?

RoboSketch-RL is a low-cost robotic arm that learns to draw shapes — a line, a circle, a square, a triangle, then handwritten digits and letters — using Reinforcement Learning (RL) and a camera. Instead of a human programming the exact motions, the robot discovers the motions on its own through trial and error, guided by how closely its drawing matches a target image.

🧠
Analogy: learning to ride a bicycle

Nobody hands you a formula for balancing a bicycle. You wobble, you fall, you feel what works, and your brain slowly tunes your muscles. RoboSketch-RL learns to draw the same way — by doing, failing, and improving — not by being handed a formula.

1.2 The key idea: "learn to draw" vs "be told how to draw" CORE IDEA

There are two ways to make a robot draw a circle:

❌ The old way (pre-programmed)

A human calculates the exact angle for every joint at every instant using geometry and inverse kinematics, and the robot blindly replays those numbers. If anything changes (the pen slips, the paper moves), it fails.

✅ RoboSketch-RL's way (learned)

The robot is given a goal ("match this picture") and a way to see its own drawing. It figures out the motions itself and can adapt. We teach it how to learn, not how to draw.

This single shift — teaching a robot how to learn to draw rather than programming how to draw — is the heart of the project and the source of its research value.

1.3 The vision of the project

The long-term vision is a cheap, general drawing robot that can reproduce any shape you show it, having learned the skill of drawing rather than memorizing one drawing. It should be built for a small budget (around ₹10,000) so that students and hobbyists — not just big labs — can explore learning-based robotics.

1.4 The final goal

The final goal has two layers:

  1. A working system: a physical 6-joint arm that watches a target image and draws it accurately.
  2. A research contribution: a scientific paper — aimed at venues like IEEE / ICRA / IROS / RA-L — showing that vision-guided RL can teach a low-cost arm to draw, and measuring how well it works. GOAL
🎯
The goal is knowledge, not just a gadget

We are not only building a robot — we are running an experiment to answer a question: "Can a low-cost arm learn to draw from visual feedback, and does learning from vision beat learning from perfect internal numbers?" That question is what makes it publishable.

1.5 Expected outcomes

  • A trained RL "brain" (a policy) that draws basic shapes in simulation.
  • The same brain transferred to a real, physical arm (sim-to-real).
  • Numbers that prove how good it is: shape overlap (IoU), error (MSE), success rate, and training time — all defined later in the book.
  • A comparison of algorithms (PPO first, then SAC) against a traditional non-learning baseline.
  • A written thesis / research paper documenting the whole journey.

1.6 Why this project is genuinely interesting

It's visible

Learning is abstract, but a robot slowly drawing a better and better circle lets you watch intelligence appear.

It's a full stack

It touches robotics, vision, AI, simulation, and hardware — a complete engineering education in one project.

It's affordable

Under ₹10,000, so the science isn't locked behind expensive lab equipment.

1.7 Research possibilities

Several honest research questions live inside this project (explored fully in Part 13):

  • Does a vision-based reward (judging the drawing from a camera) work better than a ground-truth reward (using perfect internal coordinates)?
  • Is it better for the robot to think in pen space ("move the pen here") or joint space ("rotate this motor")? One transfers to new arms more easily.
  • How well does a policy trained in simulation survive the jump to real hardware (the sim-to-real gap)?
  • Which algorithm learns to draw faster and more reliably — PPO or SAC?

1.8 Real-world applications

The skill "learn a precise, vision-guided motion" reaches far beyond drawing:

  • RoboSketch-RL = a cheap arm that learns to draw from camera feedback, not from pre-set instructions.
  • The novelty is teaching the robot how to learn to draw.
  • The end product is both a working robot and a research paper.
  • The same idea powers vision-guided manufacturing, medicine, and more.

2 Part 2 · Foundations

Robotics Basics — From Absolute Zero

 ▸

Before we can teach a robot arm to draw, we need to understand what a robot arm is and the words engineers use to describe it. This part builds that vocabulary from scratch.

2.1 What is a robot?

A robot is a machine that can sense its surroundings, decide what to do, and act on the physical world — often without a human controlling every move. Three abilities: sense → think → act. A washing machine only acts; a robot closes the loop by sensing and deciding too.

2.2 Types of robots

TypeWhat it isExample
Mobile robotsMove around a spaceRobot vacuum, delivery rover
Manipulators (arms)Stay fixed, move a "hand" in spaceFactory arm, our project
HumanoidsHuman-shaped, general purposeResearch humanoids
DronesFlyQuadcopters

RoboSketch-RL uses a manipulator — a robotic arm bolted to a table.

2.3 Robot arms, links, and joints

A robot arm copies the design of your own arm. Your arm has rigid bones connected by movable joints. In robots:

  • Link = a rigid segment (like a bone). It does not bend.
  • Joint = the movable connection between two links (like your shoulder, elbow, wrist). A motor lives here and rotates one link relative to the next.
  • Base = the fixed end bolted to the table.
  • End effector = the "hand" at the tip — the tool that does the actual job. For us, the end effector is a pen holder.

2.4 Degrees of Freedom (DOF)

Degrees of Freedom = the number of independent ways the arm can move — usually the number of motorized joints. More DOF = more flexibility to reach a point from many angles.

🕺
Analogy

Your arm has roughly 7 DOF (shoulder 3, elbow 1, wrist 3). That's why you can touch your nose with your elbow pointing up or down — same fingertip, different poses.

RoboSketch-RL uses a 6-DOF arm DESIGN CHOICE. Six is the classic number that lets an end effector reach any position and any orientation in 3D space — enough to place a pen flat on paper at any spot and angle.

2.5 The end effector (our pen holder)

The end effector is whatever tool sits at the arm's tip: a gripper, a suction cup, a welder, or — here — a pen holder that keeps a pen pointed down and pressed gently on the paper. Everything the arm does is really about putting this one point (the pen tip) in the right place.

2.6 Motors — how joints move

Joints move because motors turn them. Three important kinds:

Servo motor

A motor you command to a specific angle ("go to 45°") and it holds there. Simple, cheap, precise enough for hobby arms. We use these (MG996R).

Stepper motor

Moves in tiny fixed steps, good for precise repeatable motion (3D printers). Heavier and needs more control electronics.

Encoder

Not a motor — a sensor that measures how far a shaft actually turned, so the robot knows its true angle (feedback).

🔧
Why servos for this project DESIGN CHOICE

Servos are the cheapest way to get "go to this angle" behavior, which is exactly what a small drawing arm needs. The planned MG996R servo is a popular, strong, inexpensive hobby servo. This may change if we need more precision.

2.7 Workspace

The workspace is every point the pen tip can physically reach — a 3D "bubble" around the arm. The paper must sit inside the workspace, or the robot literally cannot touch parts of it. Choosing where to place the paper is a real design step.

2.8 Coordinate systems & Cartesian coordinates

To tell the robot where, we need numbers for locations. A coordinate system is an agreed way to turn a place into numbers. The most common is Cartesian coordinates: measure along three perpendicular axes.

  • X — left/right
  • Y — forward/back
  • Z — up/down (height)

So "the pen is at (10, 5, 0)" means 10 units right, 5 forward, at table height. On flat paper, Z is basically fixed (pen touching paper) and drawing becomes a 2D problem in (X, Y).

2.9 Forward Kinematics (FK) vs Inverse Kinematics (IK)

These two words come up constantly. They are opposites.

Forward Kinematics (FK)

Angles → position. "If each joint is at these angles, where is the pen tip?" Easy: plug angles into geometry, get one answer.

Inverse Kinematics (IK)

Position → angles. "I want the pen tip here — what angles do I need?" Harder: there may be many answers or none.

2.10 Why IK matters in this project

The RL "brain" prefers to think in simple pen terms — "move the pen a little to the right." But motors only understand angles. IK is the translator that turns "put the pen at (x, y)" into "set joints to these angles."

🔑
Why this makes the project portable DESIGN CHOICE

If the agent decides in pen space and lets IK handle angles, then swapping to a different arm only means swapping the IK — the learned brain stays the same. If instead the agent learned raw angles for one specific arm, it couldn't move to a new arm. This "pen space vs joint space" choice is itself a research question (Part 13).

💡
Good news

You don't have to solve IK by hand. PyBullet (our simulator) has a built-in function, calculateInverseKinematics, that does the math for you.

2.11 Sensors, cameras, and calibration

A sensor turns something physical into numbers the robot can use. The main sensor here is a camera, which turns light into a grid of numbers (an image). But a camera by itself doesn't know where things are in the robot's world — it just sees pixels.

Calibration is the one-time process of teaching the system how camera pixels line up with real-world positions: "the pixel at column 320, row 240 corresponds to this spot on the paper." Without calibration, the robot sees its drawing but can't relate it to where the pen actually is.

📐
Analogy

Calibration is like taping a printed grid onto a table and telling a friend "square B3 is here." Afterward, any point they name on the grid, you know its real location.

2.12 Accuracy vs precision

These sound the same but aren't:

Accuracy

How close you get to the true target. Hitting the bullseye.

Precision (repeatability)

How consistent you are. Hitting the same spot every time — even if it's the wrong spot.

A cheap arm might be precise (always ends up in the same place) but not accurate (that place is a bit off from the target). A big benefit of vision feedback is that the camera can correct for inaccuracy by watching where the pen actually went.

Key takeaways
  • A robot arm is links (bones) + joints (motors) + an end effector (our pen holder).
  • DOF = number of independent moves; we use a 6-DOF arm.
  • FK: angles→position. IK: position→angles. IK lets the brain think in pen space and stay portable.
  • A camera sees pixels; calibration ties pixels to real positions.
  • Accuracy = close to target; precision = consistent. Vision helps fix accuracy.
⚠️
Common beginner mistakes

(1) Confusing FK and IK — remember IK is the hard one we need. (2) Assuming a precise robot is automatically accurate. (3) Forgetting the paper must be inside the workspace bubble.

Chapter summary. You now speak "robot arm": links, joints, DOF, end effector, workspace, coordinates, FK/IK, sensors, calibration, and accuracy vs precision. IK is the bridge from the brain's decisions to real motor angles, and vision plus calibration is what lets a cheap arm draw accurately.

A function is a named, reusable block of instructions. You give it inputs, it does a job, maybe returns a result. It saves you from writing the same steps over and over.

def distance(x1, y1, x2, y2):

4 Part 4 · The "Eyes"

Computer Vision — Giving the Robot Eyes

 ▸

RoboSketch-RL judges its own drawing by looking at it. That looking is Computer Vision — teaching a computer to understand images. This part builds vision from a single pixel up to comparing two drawings.

4.1 What is Computer Vision?

Computer Vision (CV) is the field of getting computers to extract meaning from images and video — to answer questions like "where is the line?", "is this a circle?", "how much of the target did I cover?". A camera captures light; CV turns that light into understanding.

4.2 Pixels, images, RGB, grayscale

A digital image is just a grid of tiny squares called pixels (picture elements). Each pixel stores a color as numbers.

  • RGB: each pixel = three numbers (Red, Green, Blue), each 0–255. (255,0,0) is pure red; (0,0,0) is black; (255,255,255) is white.
  • Grayscale: each pixel = one number 0 (black) to 255 (white). No color, just brightness.
🔑
The big unlock

Because an image is just numbers, comparing two drawings becomes comparing two grids of numbers — which is exactly how we'll measure whether the robot's drawing matches the target.

4.3 Turning an image into a clean shape

A raw camera image is noisy. Vision uses a pipeline of simple operations to boil it down to the shape we care about:

Grayscale & Thresholding

First convert to grayscale (color doesn't matter for a pen line). Then threshold: pick a cutoff brightness and turn every pixel into pure black or pure white — "is this pixel part of the line, yes or no?". This gives a clean binary image.

Edge detection

Edges are places where brightness changes sharply — the borders of shapes. Edge detection (e.g. the Canny method) finds these outlines, throwing away flat areas.

Contours

A contour is a connected curve tracing the boundary of a shape — like tracing a cookie's outline with your finger. Finding contours turns a blob of pixels into an actual shape you can measure (length, area, is-it-closed?).

4.4 Camera calibration & perspective transformation

A camera looking at paper from an angle sees a skewed shape (a rectangle looks like a trapezoid). Perspective transformation is a math trick that "un-skews" the image, as if you were looking straight down. Combined with calibration (Part 2.11), this lets the robot map any point in the photo to a real spot on the paper.

🖼️
Analogy

Perspective transform is like taking a photo of a slanted poster and digitally straightening it into a perfect flat rectangle.

4.5 Shape detection & image comparison

Shape detection asks "is this contour a circle, square, or triangle?" by measuring corners and curves. Image comparison asks "how similar are these two images?" — the core of scoring a drawing. Two common measures (defined fully in Part 10):

  • Pixel overlap / IoU — of all the pixels that should be dark, how many actually are? How much do the two shapes overlap?
  • MSE (Mean Squared Error) — on average, how different is each pixel from the target?

4.6 OpenCV — the vision toolbox

OpenCV (Open Source Computer Vision Library) is the world's most popular free library for computer vision. Every operation above — grayscale, threshold, edges, contours, perspective transform, comparison — is a ready-made OpenCV function. FIXED TOOL

JobOpenCV function (example)
Read/capture an imagecv2.imread, cv2.VideoCapture

What OpenCV does in RoboSketch-RL

That final similarity number becomes the reward the RL agent learns from. So OpenCV is quite literally the robot's judge of its own work.

Key takeaways
  • An image is a grid of number-valued pixels; that's why math can "see."
  • The vision pipeline: grayscale → threshold → edges → contours → compare.
  • Calibration + perspective transform map camera pixels to real paper positions.
  • OpenCV provides every one of these as a ready function; its output becomes the reward.
⚠️
Common beginner mistake

Skipping calibration and wondering why the robot "sees" the drawing but can't tell where it is. Also: bad lighting ruins thresholding — vision needs steady, even light.

Chapter summary. Computer vision turns pictures into numbers and numbers into understanding. Using OpenCV, RoboSketch-RL converts a camera frame into a clean shape, maps it to real paper coordinates, and compares it to the target — producing the similarity score that drives learning.
5 Part 5 · The "Brain"

Reinforcement Learning — From Zero

 ▸

This is the heart of the project. We'll go from "what is AI?" all the way to PPO — the algorithm the robot will likely use — with simple examples first, then connect every idea back to drawing.

5.1 AI, Machine Learning, Deep Learning, RL — how they nest

These four words are often mixed up. They fit inside each other like nested boxes:

  • AI (Artificial Intelligence): any machine that does something "smart."
  • ML (Machine Learning): AI that learns from data/experience instead of being fully hand-coded.
  • DL (Deep Learning): ML using neural networks — math models loosely inspired by the brain, great at patterns in images and signals.
  • RL (Reinforcement Learning): ML that learns from trial and error and rewards — learning by doing, not from a fixed answer sheet.

5.2 The one idea behind RL: learn from reward

In Reinforcement Learning, a learner tries actions, receives a reward (a score) for how good the outcome was, and gradually shifts toward actions that earn more reward. Over thousands of tries it discovers a strategy — no one ever tells it the correct action, only how good its result was.

🐶
Analogy: training a dog

You don't explain "sit" in words. When the dog happens to sit, it gets a treat (reward). More treats for sitting → the dog sits more. RL is training software the same way, with points instead of treats.

5.3 The RL vocabulary (the words you must know)

Every RL problem is described with the same handful of words. Learn these once and they apply everywhere.

TermPlain meaningIn RoboSketch-RL
AgentThe learner / decision-makerThe "brain" controlling the arm
EnvironmentThe world the agent acts inThe arm + paper + camera (the simulation)
State (observation)What the agent currently knows/seesPen position, target image, current drawing
ActionA move the agent can makeNudge the pen (dx,dy), or pen up/down
RewardA number scoring the outcome+ for matching the target, − for going off-paper
EpisodeOne full attempt, start to finishOne try at drawing the shape
PolicyThe agent's strategy: state → actionThe trained brain we keep at the end

5.4 Exploration vs Exploitation

A learner faces a dilemma every step:

Exploitation

Do the move you already know is good. Safe, but you might miss something better.

Exploration

Try something new and unknown. Risky, but it's the only way to discover better moves.

🍕
Analogy

Ordering your favorite pizza every time (exploit) vs trying a new dish (explore). Too much exploiting = you never find a better favorite. Too much exploring = you keep eating bad meals. Good RL balances both.

5.5 Value and Q-value

Some situations are worth more than others because they lead to more future reward.

  • Value of a state = "how much total reward do I expect from here on?" A pen near the target line has high value.
  • Q-value = value of taking a specific action in a specific state: "how good is moving right, from here?"

Learning good Q-values basically means learning "which move is best in each situation" — which is the policy.

5.6 The algorithms: DQN, PPO, SAC

An RL algorithm is a specific recipe for learning the policy from experience. Three important ones:

AlgorithmShort forBest when actions are…One-line idea
DQNDeep Q-NetworkDiscrete (up/down/left/right)Learn a Q-value for each possible action.
PPOProximal Policy OptimizationContinuous (smooth amounts)Improve the policy in small, safe steps so training stays stable.
SACSoft Actor-CriticContinuousLearn efficiently while staying curious (rewards exploration).

5.7 Why PPO is likely first for RoboSketch-RL DESIGN CHOICE

Drawing needs continuous actions — the pen can move a little or a lot in any direction, not just fixed grid steps. That rules out plain DQN (built for discrete choices) and points to PPO or SAC.

PPO is planned first because it is famously stable and forgiving — it's the default "reliable workhorse" of modern RL, hard to break, and well-supported by our library (Stable-Baselines3). Once PPO works, we'll try SAC and compare which learns to draw faster and better. This comparison is part of the research. This ordering can change if experiments say otherwise.

🔑
Why compare algorithms at all?

A paper claiming "RL can teach drawing" is stronger if it shows the result isn't a fluke of one algorithm. Comparing PPO vs SAC (and vs a non-learning baseline) is what turns a demo into science.

5.8 Trial-and-error learning, tied to drawing

Let's connect everything with the concrete example of drawing a line:

RL conceptDrawing a line
AgentThe policy controlling the arm
StateWhere the pen is now + the target line + what's drawn so far
ActionMove the pen a small step in some direction
Reward+ when the pen stays on the target line and covers new parts of it; − when it wanders off the paper or off the line
EpisodeOne full attempt to draw the line (ends when done, or after ~300 steps)
Policy (result)After many episodes: "given the pen here and the target there, move like this" — a robot that draws lines
🔁
Learning happens by repetition

A single episode teaches almost nothing. The magic comes from running millions of episodes — which is only affordable in simulation (Part 6), where attempts are free and instant, not in the real world where each try wastes paper and time.

Key takeaways
  • RL is learning by trial, error, and reward — no answer sheet.
  • Master the 7 words: agent, environment, state, action, reward, episode, policy.
  • Balance exploration (try new) and exploitation (use what works).
  • Drawing needs continuous actions → PPO first, then SAC (a design choice we'll test).
  • Learning needs millions of tries → we train in simulation.
⚠️
Common beginner mistakes

(1) Expecting fast results — RL needs huge numbers of tries. (2) Designing a reward that accidentally encourages the wrong behavior (Part 10 is all about avoiding this). (3) Confusing the policy (the final brain) with the algorithm (the recipe that trains it).

Chapter summary. Reinforcement Learning teaches an agent to act well by rewarding good outcomes over countless trials. With the seven core terms and the explore/exploit balance in hand, you understand how RoboSketch-RL's brain learns to draw — using PPO first, likely SAC next, trained in simulation where practice is unlimited.

 ▸

RoboSketch-RL learns inside a computer before any real robot exists. This part explains why, and introduces PyBullet — the physics playground where the robot practices.

6.1 Why simulation comes before hardware STRATEGY

RL needs millions of attempts. Doing that on a real arm would be a disaster:

🔑
Golden rule of this project

Do not buy hardware until the simulation and learning work. Almost all debugging happens in sim. Hardware comes only when the brain already knows how to draw.

6.2 Digital Twin

A digital twin is a virtual copy of a real object that behaves like the real thing. Our simulated arm is a digital twin of the physical arm we'll build later: same joints, same lengths, same limits. Train the twin, then copy the learned brain onto the real one.

6.3 Physics engine

For a simulation to be useful, virtual objects must obey real physics — gravity, collisions, the pen pressing on paper. A physics engine is software that calculates these forces and motions many times per second, so the virtual world behaves believably.

🎮
Analogy

It's the same technology that makes objects in video games fall, bounce, and collide realistically — but here we use it for serious engineering practice.

6.4 PyBullet — our simulator TOOL

PyBullet is a free Python physics-simulation library built for robotics and RL research. It's popular because it's lightweight, controllable from Python, and comes with sample robot arms ready to use.

Why we chose it

Free, Python-native, robotics-focused, has built-in inverse kinematics, and ships with tested arms (like the KUKA iiwa) so we can start building the drawing engine immediately.

Current sim arm

The project starts with the bundled KUKA iiwa arm DESIGN CHOICE — zero download, well-tested IK — then swaps to a hardware-matched low-cost arm at the sim-to-real stage.

6.5 What we simulate

🦾 The robot

Load the arm from a description file (URDF) — its joints, links, and limits — and command its motors.

📷 A virtual camera

PyBullet can render what a camera would see, so we can run the exact vision pipeline in sim, camera and all.

✏️ The drawing

Track where the pen tip touches the paper and record each stroke, building up the drawn image.

📄
What's a URDF?

URDF (Unified Robot Description Format) is a text file that describes a robot's body: how many joints, how long each link is, how far each joint can rotate. Loading a different URDF = simulating a different arm. Swapping arms later mainly means swapping this file.

6.6 Collision detection

Collision detection is the engine noticing when two things touch — e.g. the pen tip meeting the paper, or the arm bumping itself. It's how the sim knows "the pen is now drawing" (pen touching paper) versus "the pen is in the air."

6.7 Training inside simulation

Put it together: the RL agent (Part 5) acts inside the PyBullet world, the virtual camera + OpenCV judge the drawing, a reward is computed, and the agent improves — all at computer speed, for free, safely, millions of times. When it draws well in sim, we move to real hardware.

PyBullet world→ Agent acts→ Virtual camera + OpenCV→ Reward→ Agent improves↺

6.8 The sim-to-real gap CONCEPT

Simulation is never perfectly real — the real pen might drag, the real motors might lag, the real paper might shift. The difference between "works in sim" and "works in reality" is the sim-to-real gap. Shrinking this gap (e.g. by adding realistic randomness during training) is a whole research topic and a key challenge when we move to hardware in Part 8.

Key takeaways
  • RL needs millions of tries → practice in simulation first (free, fast, safe).
  • A digital twin in a physics engine (PyBullet) mimics the real arm.
  • We simulate the arm (from a URDF), a virtual camera, the drawing, and collisions.
  • Don't buy hardware until the sim works. Beware the sim-to-real gap.
⚠️
Common beginner mistake

Rushing to buy hardware first. Also: trusting a policy that works in a too-perfect sim — if the sim ignores real-world messiness, the robot fails in reality. Add realistic noise during training.

7.3 Two versions of the reward loop RESEARCH DESIGN

An important design detail: the "drawing image" feeding the reward can come from two sources, and comparing them is part of the research.

① Ground-truth reward (baseline first)

Use the simulator's perfect knowledge of the pen tip's position — no camera needed. Simple, exact. Built first as the control/baseline.

② Vision reward (proposed method)

One research question is "does learning from vision work as well as learning from perfect internal numbers?" To answer it scientifically you need both versions to compare.   1. You show the robot a target shape.
  2. The camera photographs the (currently blank) paper.
  3. OpenCV extracts what's drawn so far.
  4. The system builds a state from that plus the pen position and target.
  5. The RL agent picks an action — where to move the pen next.
  6. IK turns that into joint angles; the arm moves and draws.
  7. The camera sees the new drawing; vision computes a reward.
  8. The agent learns from the reward, and the loop repeats — getting a little better each time.

8.2 Each component in detail

🦾 6-DOF robot arm

What: the mechanical arm — six motorized joints, links, and a base — that carries the pen. Why: it's the body; without it there's no drawing. Alternatives: 4-DOF arm (cheaper, less flexible), or a 2-axis pen plotter (much simpler but not really a "learning arm"). Why 6-DOF: enough freedom to place the pen at any point and angle, matching what we simulate. DESIGN CHOICE

⚙️ Servo motors (MG996R)

What: "go-to-this-angle" motors that live in the joints. Why: they physically move the arm. Alternatives: smaller SG90 servos (cheaper, weaker — fine for tiny arms), or stepper motors (more precise, more complex/expensive). Why MG996R: a popular, strong, inexpensive hobby servo with enough torque to hold the arm steady while drawing.

🎛️ Arduino Uno (controller)

What: a small, beginner-friendly microcontroller board — a tiny computer that reads commands and outputs electrical signals to motors. Why: the laptop (running Python/RL) can't drive motors directly; the Arduino is the middle-man that turns "set joint 3 to 40°" into real servo signals. Alternatives: ESP32 (adds Wi-Fi/Bluetooth, more power), Raspberry Pi (a full Linux computer — overkill unless you want the vision/RL running on-board). Why Arduino Uno: cheapest, simplest, best-documented for beginners driving servos.

🔌
Arduino vs ESP32 vs Raspberry Pi

Arduino Uno = simple motor controller (no OS). ESP32 = like Arduino but with wireless and more speed. Raspberry Pi = a real computer that could run Python, OpenCV, even the RL policy by itself. For this project the laptop does the thinking and the Arduino does the moving, so Arduino is enough. A Pi becomes attractive only if you later want a standalone robot with no laptop attached.

🔀 PCA9685 servo driver

What: a small board that can precisely control up to 16 servos over just two control wires. Why: six servos need six clean, steady signals; the Arduino alone struggles to drive that many precisely, and the driver offloads that job. Alternatives: drive servos directly from Arduino pins (works for a couple of servos, gets jittery with six). Why PCA9685: cheap, reliable, made exactly for driving many servos smoothly.

📷 USB webcam

What: an ordinary camera that plugs into the laptop. Why: it's the robot's eye — it photographs the paper so OpenCV can score the drawing (closing the vision loop). Alternatives: a Raspberry Pi camera (if using a Pi), or a phone camera. Why a USB webcam: plug-and-play with OpenCV on a laptop, cheap, and "good enough" resolution for line drawings.

🔋 Power supply

What: a dedicated 5–6V power source rated for several amps (≥5A). Why: servos draw a lot of current, especially six moving at once — far more than a USB port can give. A weak supply causes brownouts, jitter, and resets. Alternatives: a battery pack (portable but needs monitoring). Why a wall adapter: steady, cheap, no recharging.

⚡
Safety & power

Never power six servos from the Arduino/USB alone — use the separate supply, and connect all grounds together. Under-powering is the #1 cause of a jittery, misbehaving hobby arm.

✏️ Pen holder (end effector) & drawing board

What: a mount at the arm's tip that grips a pen and keeps it pointing down; plus a flat, clamped board holding the paper. Why: the pen must press with steady, consistent pressure and the paper must not shift, or the vision feedback becomes unreliable. Alternatives: a spring-loaded holder (keeps constant pressure — often better), 3D-printed custom holder. Why: simple, cheap, and pen pressure strongly affects drawing quality.

🔩 Wires, bearings, screws, mounts

What: the small mechanical and electrical parts that hold everything together and let joints turn smoothly (bearings reduce friction). Why: a wobbly, loose arm draws badly no matter how smart the brain is. Often included in an arm kit.

10 Part 10 · The Secret Sauce

Reward Design — Teaching Through Points

 ▸

The reward is how we tell the robot "good job" or "no, worse." It is arguably the most important and most delicate design decision in the whole project — get it wrong and the robot learns the wrong thing.

10.1 Why reward design is everything

In RL, the agent will do whatever earns the most reward — even if that's not what you meant. The reward function is the only channel through which you communicate the goal. So designing it well is like writing very careful instructions to a genie who takes you literally.

🧞
Analogy: the literal genie

Ask for "the most ink on the paper" and the robot might scribble a solid black rectangle — technically lots of ink, totally wrong. The reward must capture what you actually want: a drawing that matches the target.

10.2 The building blocks of the reward PROPOSED DESIGN

The final reward is usually a sum of several smaller rewards and penalties, each nudging a specific behavior. Here are the pieces under consideration — remember these are proposed and will be tuned through experiments.

Reward piece+ or −What it encourages / discouragesWhy include it
Shape similarity / pixel overlap (IoU)+Making the drawing match the targetThe main goal — this is what "good drawing" means
Distance reduction+Moving the pen closer to where it should beGives helpful hints early, before any shape exists
Smooth movement+Gentle, steady motionsJerky motion draws messy lines and stresses real servos
Pen stability+Keeping consistent pen pressure/contactSteady pressure = clean, even lines
Completion bonus+ (big)Finishing the whole shapeA clear "you did it!" signal to aim for
Off-paper / off-line penalty−Leaving the paper or straying from the targetStops wandering and scribbling
Time / step penalty− (small)Taking too longEncourages efficient, direct drawing
Mistake penalty−Drawing where it shouldn'tDiscourages extra, wrong strokes

10.3 The two headline metrics: IoU and MSE METRICS

Two ways to measure "how close is the drawing to the target" — used both inside the reward and to report results in the paper.

IoU — Intersection over Union

Of the area covered by drawing and target combined, how much do they overlap? Range 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect). Higher = better. Great for "did the shapes line up?"

MSE — Mean Squared Error

On average, how different is each pixel from the target? Lower = better (0 = identical). Punishes big differences strongly.

10.4 Sparse vs dense rewards

A key design tension:

Sparse reward

Only reward at the very end ("+100 if the final drawing matches"). Honest, but the robot gets almost no guidance along the way — like studying a whole year for one final grade with no feedback. Learning is slow.

Dense reward

Reward small progress every step (closer, on the line, smooth). Learns much faster because feedback is constant — but you must design it carefully so the pieces don't reward the wrong thing.

RoboSketch-RL leans toward a dense reward (progress + overlap + penalties) for faster learning, with a completion bonus at the end. DESIGN CHOICE

10.5 Reward hacking — the classic trap

🎭
Reward hacking

When the agent finds a loophole that scores high without doing the real task. E.g. if "ink on paper" is rewarded, it scribbles. If "time" is over-penalized, it rushes and draws nothing. Every reward piece must be checked: "could the robot cheat this?" Designing, testing, and fixing the reward is an experimental cycle, not a one-time guess.

10.6 How reward design becomes research

Because the reward strongly shapes behavior, comparing different reward designs is itself a scientific result: ground-truth vs vision-based reward, sparse vs dense, with vs without a smoothness term. Each comparison can be a figure in the paper showing what makes learning-to-draw work.

Key takeaways
  • The reward is the only way you tell the robot what "good" means — design it like instructions to a literal genie.
  • It's usually a sum of pieces: overlap (IoU), progress, smoothness, stability, completion bonus, minus penalties.
  • IoU (overlap, higher better) and MSE (error, lower better) measure drawing quality.
  • Dense rewards learn faster than sparse; beware reward hacking.
  • Comparing reward designs is a real research contribution.
⚠️
Common beginner mistake

Assuming the first reward you write is correct. It almost never is — expect to run, watch what the robot actually optimizes, and fix the reward several times. That loop is the work.

Chapter summary. The reward function is the project's most influential and most easily mis-designed piece. RoboSketch-RL combines a shape-overlap reward (IoU) with progress, smoothness, stability, and completion bonuses, minus penalties for wandering and wasting time — a dense reward that must be repeatedly tested against reward hacking. Comparing reward designs is part of the science.

13 Part 13 · Why It's Science

Research Perspective — What Could Be Novel

 ▸

RoboSketch-RL isn't just a cool build — it's aimed at a publication. This part explains what "research contribution" means, what's already known, and where the new ideas might live.

13.1 What makes something a research contribution?

A research contribution answers a question nobody has clearly answered before, and does it carefully enough that others can trust and reproduce the result. It's not "I built a robot" — it's "I learned something new about how to build robots, and here's the evidence."

13.2 What's already known (not novel)

  • Robot arms and inverse kinematics — decades old.
  • Reinforcement Learning, PPO, SAC — well-established methods.
  • OpenCV image processing — standard.
  • Robots that draw using pre-programmed paths — done many times.

Using these isn't new. The contribution has to be in how we combine and study them.

13.3 What might be novel here

Learning to draw, not programming to draw

Teaching a low-cost arm to draw via vision-guided RL instead of hand-coded trajectories — and showing it works on a small budget.

Vision reward vs ground-truth reward

Directly testing whether learning from a camera (like the real world) matches learning from perfect internal coordinates.

Pen-space vs joint-space actions

Measuring which representation transfers better across arms and survives sim-to-real.

Low-cost sim-to-real for drawing

How well a policy trained in sim performs on a ~₹10,000 arm — a practical, reproducible benchmark.

⚗️
Honesty note

Whether these are truly "novel" depends on what already exists in the literature — that's what a literature review checks. The value may lie less in a brand-new method and more in a careful, reproducible, low-cost study of these questions. Both are legitimate contributions.

13.4 Hypotheses to test

LabelHypothesisHow to test
H1A low-cost arm can learn to draw basic shapes with vision-guided RL.Train & measure IoU/MSE/success rate.
H2A vision-based reward works as well as (or better than) a ground-truth reward.Train both versions, compare results.
H3Pen-space actions transfer to hardware better than joint-space actions.Train both, test sim-to-real gap.
H4One algorithm (PPO vs SAC) learns faster/more reliably for this task.Train both, compare curves & final scores.

13.5 Measuring results (the evidence)

Claims need numbers. The planned metrics:

  • IoU — shape overlap with the target (higher better).
  • MSE — average pixel error (lower better).
  • Success rate — fraction of attempts that reach a "good enough" drawing.
  • Training time / sample efficiency — how many tries to learn.

And a baseline for comparison: a traditional IK + trajectory-planning drawer (no learning). Beating or matching it — while being more adaptable — is the story.

13.6 Future improvements & publication venues

Beyond the first paper, extensions could include multi-color drawing, more complex artwork, faster drawing, or transfer to different arms. Suitable venues for robotics + learning work include IEEE journals/conferences such as ICRA, IROS, RA-L, and CoRL. TARGET

  • A contribution = a new, carefully-evidenced answer to a question — not just a working build.
  • The tools (RL, IK, OpenCV) are known; the novelty is in the learning-to-draw approach and the comparisons.
  • Test clear hypotheses (H1–H4) with real metrics (IoU, MSE, success rate, training time) against a non-learning baseline.

Cartesian coordinates
Locating a point by distances along X, Y, Z axes.
Collision detection
The simulator noticing when two objects touch (e.g. pen meets paper).
Contour
A connected curve tracing the outline of a shape in an image.
Degrees of Freedom (DOF)
The number of independent ways a robot can move; usually its joint count.
Dense reward
Reward given for small progress every step (vs only at the end).
Digital twin
A virtual copy of a real object that behaves like it.
Edge detection
Finding where brightness changes sharply — the borders of shapes.
End effector
The tool at the arm's tip; here, the pen holder.
Environment
The world the RL agent acts in (arm + paper + camera).
Episode
One complete attempt, from start to finish.
Exploration / Exploitation
Trying new actions vs using known-good ones.
Forward Kinematics (FK)
Computing pen position from joint angles.
Grayscale
An image using only brightness (0–255), no color.
Inverse Kinematics (IK)
Computing joint angles needed to place the pen at a target position.
IoU (Intersection over Union)
Overlap between two shapes ÷ their combined area; 0–1, higher better.
Joint
A motorized movable connection between two links.
Link
A rigid segment of the arm (like a bone).
Machine Learning (ML)
AI that learns from data/experience rather than being fully hand-coded.
MSE (Mean Squared Error)
Average squared difference between drawing and target pixels; lower better.
Neural network
A brain-inspired math model good at learning patterns.
Physics engine
Software that computes realistic forces, motion, and collisions.
Pixel
One tiny colored square of an image, stored as numbers.
Policy
The agent's strategy mapping states to actions — the trained brain.
PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)
A stable RL algorithm for continuous actions; the project's first choice.
Precision (repeatability)
How consistently a result is reproduced, right or wrong.
Reward
A number scoring how good an outcome was.
Reward hacking
The agent exploiting a loophole to score high without doing the real task.
RGB
Color as Red, Green, Blue values (0–255 each).
SAC (Soft Actor-Critic)
A sample-efficient RL algorithm that rewards exploration; the second choice.
Servo motor
A motor commanded to a specific angle and holds it.
Sim-to-real gap
The performance difference between simulation and the real world.
Sparse reward
Reward given only at the end of an episode.
State (observation)
What the agent currently knows/sees.
Stepper motor
A motor that moves in fixed steps for precise positioning.
Threshold(ing)
Turning a grayscale image into pure black/white by a cutoff.
URDF
A text file describing a robot's body (joints, links, limits).
Value / Q-value
Expected future reward from a state / from a state-action pair.
Workspace
Every point the end effector can physically reach.

14.4 Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to be good at math?
You need school-level math to start. The hard math (kinematics, RL updates) is handled by libraries — you use them, you don't derive them. Understanding concepts matters more than doing the calculus by hand.
Do I need to buy the robot to begin?
No — and you shouldn't yet. Everything up to trained behavior happens in simulation on your laptop. Hardware comes only after the sim works.
How long will this take?
It's a serious, months-long project — that's normal for research. Progress in small phases (Part 12), and celebrate each milestone.
What computer do I need?
A regular laptop (Windows works) runs PyBullet and light RL fine for basic shapes. Training is faster with a GPU, but not required to start.
Why does the robot need a camera if the simulator already knows where the pen is?
The real robot won't have perfect internal knowledge — it must judge its drawing by looking, like a person. Training with vision prepares it for reality, and comparing vision vs perfect-knowledge is a research question (H2).
What if my reward makes the robot do something weird?
That's reward hacking, and it's expected. Watch what it actually optimizes, then fix the reward. Iterating on the reward is a core part of the work (Part 10).
Will the choices in this book definitely be the final ones?
No. Only the research problem and purpose are fixed. Algorithm, arm, reward, and ordering are design choices that will evolve through experiments — that's how research works.
Is PPO or SAC better?
We start with PPO because it's stable and reliable, then try SAC and compare. Which is "better" for drawing is literally one of the things this project measures (H4).
